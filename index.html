Project Overview

Dual-Track Environmental & Atmospheric Research in East Texas

UTTUA GLOBE is a student-led environmental research initiative based in the Greater Tyler, Texas area. Our work combines ground-truth field observations with Texas infrastructure filings and NASA satellite telemetry across two active research tracks:

Track 1 · Texas AI Datacenter Impact

Compares Open-Loop (Evaporative) vs. Closed-Loop (Liquid/Dry) cooling across 22 Texas campuses—evaluating Before vs. After water use, hyperscale land acreage (up to 1,200+ acres), and how dry-cooled heat convection interferes with low-altitude flying.

Track 2 · NASA PACE × GLOBE Cloud Validation

Evaluates agreement between NASA's PACE satellite (OCI, HARP2, SPEXone) and GLOBE ground-based sky observations across 432 global matchups (190 Greater Tyler) from 2024 through November 19, 2026.

Sources: UTTUA GLOBE Research Archive (2024–2026), Texas Water Development Board, ERCOT Large Flexible Load Queue, NASA OB.DAAC, and GLOBE Program ADAT.

Research Focus

Project Overview & Hypothesis: Water Savings vs. Thermal Plumes

Across 22 Texas hyperscale AI campuses evaluated against their pre-construction baselines, Open-Loop (Evaporative) cooling systems drive substantial local aquifer stress and municipal water draw (averaging hundreds of millions of gallons per year), whereas Closed-Loop (Liquid/Dry) architectures cut direct operational water use by up to ~99%—trading evaporative water loss for higher dry sensible heat plumes, localized heat island spikes, and peak ERCOT electrical demand during Texas summers.

Sources: Texas Water Development Board (TWDB), ERCOT Large Flexible Load Task Force (2025–2026), and U.S. DOE Data Center Water & Energy Reports.

Airspace & Boundary Layer Physics

How Heat Convection Interferes with Flying

A 300–500 MW hyperscale datacenter rejects nearly 100% of its electrical power as waste heat into the air directly above its roof. Especially in Closed-Loop dry-cooled facilities, concentrated dry exhaust drives buoyant thermal updraft plumes rising 500 to 2,000+ ft AGL, disturbing low-altitude flight paths near regional airports, medical heliports, and drone corridors.

Airspace & Convective Plume Zone
Low-Altitude Aviation & Thermal Convection Zone
Source: Unsplash Aviation Photography · FAA Thermal Plume Siting Advisory
Thermal Updrafts: 500–2,000+ ft AGL Vertical Velocity: 4–10+ m/s Convective Wind Shear Density Altitude Spike
1. Buoyant Thermal Plumes (500–2,000+ ft AGL)

Concentrated rooftop heat rejection generates rising columns of warm air reaching 2,000+ ft AGL, abruptly lifting light aircraft, helicopters, and UAS/drones crossing final approach corridors.

2. Convective Wind Shear & Turbulence

Sharp vertical velocity gradients (4–10+ m/s) along the plume shear boundary cause sudden roll, pitch, and airspeed excursions during low-altitude traffic pattern maneuvers.

3. Density Altitude & Reduced Lift

Superheated exhaust lowers local air density directly above dry-cooler arrays, momentarily reducing wing lift, rotor thrust, and climb performance on hot Texas afternoons.

4. Moisture Plumes, Icing & FAA Siting

Evaporative cooling plumes can create localized fog or winter rime icing, prompting FAA aeronautical review for high-heat exhaust siting near public-use runways and heliports.

Sources: FAA Thermal Plume Siting Guidance (DOT/FAA/TC-TN), NOAA/NWS Convective Boundary Layer Physics, and ACRP exhaust plume studies.

Research Question & Hypothesis

Research Question: How does cloud type and cloud-cover condition affect agreement between NASA PACE satellite observations and GLOBE ground-based cloud observations?

Hypothesis: Agreement between PACE satellite-derived cloud observations and GLOBE ground-based observations will decrease during rapidly changing and vertically developed cloud conditions, particularly those associated with thunderstorms, compared with more uniform cloud conditions.

Sources: 2026 GLOBE PACE Clouds Challenge Protocol (NASA GSFC) & GLOBE Clouds Student Project Support Framework.
Scientific Background

OCI, HARP2, SPEXone & Why Top-Down vs. Bottom-Up Views Disagree

Launched on February 8, 2024, NASA's PACE satellite carries the hyperspectral OCI (~1 km spatial resolution) alongside two multi-angle polarimeters, HARP2 and SPEXone. Official automated GLOBE–PACE satellite matching began in August 2026. Disagreements between orbital retrievals and ground observers stem from obscuration bias (high cirrus hidden above low overcast decks or low clouds hidden beneath upper layers), sub-pixel cumulus smaller than OCI's 1 km footprint, and temporal mismatch during rapid convective thunderstorm growth within minutes of satellite overpass. This validation archive tracks 432 matchups worldwide—including 190 Greater Tyler Area observations—through November 19, 2026.

Sources: NASA PACE Mission Science Team (GSFC), OB.DAAC Level-2 Cloud Products, and GLOBE Observer Clouds Protocol.
